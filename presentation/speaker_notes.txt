SLIDE 1
Say (25–30 seconds): We are Team Outliers. Our project asks a simple question: when quantum measurements are limited, can information about their uncertainty help a model predict better? We compare four ways of fitting the same quantum features. The main finding is that more measurements helped, but the more detailed uncertainty penalty did not consistently beat Ridge.

If asked: The formal title matches the report. This is a semester-scale implementation and evaluation of established principles.

SLIDE 2
Say (45–50 seconds): A quantum measurement gives a random outcome. We repeat the circuit many times to estimate a feature; each repetition is a shot. With a limited number of shots, repeating the measurement process gives slightly different feature estimates. A regression model then uses these features to make a prediction. We were interested in whether knowing more about that uncertainty would improve predictions. Instead of assuming covariance regularization would help, we compared it with a well-tuned Ridge model.

If asked: Shot noise occurs even in an ideal circuit. Hardware errors are a separate issue and are not included here. Sources: Kreplin and Roth, Quantum 8, 1385 (2024), DOI 10.22331/q-2024-06-25-1385; Hu et al., PRX 13, 041020 (2023), DOI 10.1103/PhysRevX.13.041020.

SLIDE 3
Say (55–60 seconds): This is our fixed two-qubit circuit. The rotation gates encode the input, while CNOT and CZ let the qubits interact. We do not train these gates. We measure in three settings: X, Y and Z. Each setting gives two individual features and one pair feature, making nine in total. We test 32, 128 and 512 shots per setting. Since there are three settings, the total cost per input is three times that number. We predict two known mathematical functions using twenty datasets, each with five measurement repeats. Each dataset has 64 training, 32 validation and 128 test inputs.

If asked: x is uniform on [-pi,pi]. An XX shot supplies X0, X1 and their product; YY and ZZ work similarly. All state evolution and measurement sampling run on a CPU. Source: src/qml_project/quantum.py; configs/measurement_extension.json.

SLIDE 4
Say (55–60 seconds): All four models receive the same measured features. OLS simply fits the training data. Ridge discourages large weights, which can reduce sensitivity to noise. DiagonalCov also uses how uncertain each measured feature is. FullCov goes one step further and includes how features fluctuate together. Variance describes one feature's fluctuation; covariance describes shared fluctuation between two features. We estimate this uncertainty from training measurements only. The validation set chooses the penalties, and the test set is used to measure performance.

If asked: The objective is training MSE + lambda times squared weight norm + gamma times w-transpose C w. The intercept is unpenalized. C is conditional measurement-noise covariance, not across-input feature covariance. Each regularized method has 32 candidates, although grids and family complexity differ. Gamma zero switches the covariance penalty off. Covariance regularization already exists; this is an adaptation. Sources: src/measurement_extension/models.py and covariance.py; Baumann et al., arXiv:2604.28160v2, Section III-C.

SLIDE 5
Say (50–55 seconds): These graphs show average prediction error on unseen inputs. Lower is better. The left graph is sine and the right is mixed harmonics; their vertical ranges are different. Reading left to right, more shots gave lower error for every method in our primary evaluation. The four curves almost overlap, so the differences between methods are much smaller than the effect of the measurement budget. More detailed uncertainty information did not automatically produce a better prediction.

If asked: MSE is the average squared difference between prediction and target. These slides show clean-label means only. We average five acquisitions within each dataset, then average twenty dataset means. The report provides descriptive sample SDs; these are not confidence intervals or significance tests. Source: results/measurement_extension/summary.json, clean-label finite-shot rows.

SLIDE 6
Say (50–55 seconds): This chart makes the small FullCov–Ridge differences easier to see. A bar to the right means FullCov has lower mean error; a bar to the left means it has higher mean error. Only one of the six primary cases improves: mixed harmonics at 512 shots, by about 0.71 percent. Our conclusion is that FullCov did not consistently improve on Ridge in this setup. We produced a working and checked comparison, including the cases where the added penalty failed to help. Next, we could test a more stable covariance estimate on fresh data.

If asked: The six cases are two targets times three shot budgets, not six datasets. FullCov beats the diagonal ablation in three of six primary means. FullCov selects gamma zero in 43–57% of primary fits. The separate noisy-label sensitivity has four of six improvements and does not replace the primary result. A shrinkage estimator is future work, not an implemented result. This is a two-qubit synthetic CPU study, with no quantum-advantage or statistical-significance claim. Recorded checks include 54 tests, independent audits and reproduction. Sources: results/measurement_extension/summary.json; docs/EXTENSION_RESULTS.md.
